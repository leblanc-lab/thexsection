Two precision results carry the day, from opposite ends of the field. ATLAS posts its full Run 2 Higgs combination [arXiv:2608.07332](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07332) — inclusive signal strength $0.990^{+0.053}_{-0.051}$, now signal-theory-limited, plus $\kappa_\lambda=1.3^{+3.1}_{-1.6}$, $\Gamma_H=3.6^{+2.1}_{-1.6}$ MeV, and a first ATLAS extraction of the running $b$-mass at $m_H$. ## Deep dives

### [arXiv:2608.07332](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07332) — Combination of Higgs boson measurements at √s = 13 TeV and their interpretations by the ATLAS experiment (ATLAS Collaboration)

*(Abstract only available.)* ATLAS combines its full Run 2 (up to 140 fb⁻¹) Higgs measurements into a single reference, reporting an inclusive signal strength of $0.990^{+0.053}_{-0.051}$ — split as $\pm0.027$ (stat.), $\pm0.024$ (exp.), $^{+0.037}_{-0.035}$ (sig. theo.), $^{+0.016}_{-0.015}$ (bkg. theo.). The signal-theory term, dominated by missing higher-order QCD and parton-shower modelling, is now the leading uncertainty, so precision is theory-limited rather than statistics-limited. Sensitivities improve up to 35% over the previous combination. Combining single-Higgs and $HH$ rates gives $\kappa_\lambda=1.3^{+3.1}_{-1.6}$ ($[-1.5,6.5]$ at 95% CL); the on-/off-shell rates yield $\Gamma_H=3.6^{+2.1}_{-1.6}$ MeV; and the $H\to b\bar b$ partial width delivers a first ATLAS running $b$-mass at the Higgs scale, $m_b(m_H)=2.73^{+0.27}_{-0.25}$ GeV (MSbar). Kinematics are probed via STXS in 44 regions and interpreted in SMEFT.

Why it matters: this is the document every future coupling, self-coupling, and width comparison will cite, closing out the incremental $HH$/$tH$ updates the memory has tracked. The signal strength sitting on unity at percent-level with theory now dominant sharpens where the community must invest — better fixed-order and shower predictions rather than more luminosity. The $b$-mass extraction demonstrates the Higgs as a running-mass probe. Caveats are structural: $\kappa_\lambda$ and $\Gamma_H$ remain broad, and the width relies on the on-/off-shell interpretation with its model assumptions.

### [arXiv:2608.06480](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06480) — Cosmic birefringence from a joint analysis of ACT and Planck (Johannes R. Eskilt)

Eskilt performs the first joint isotropic-birefringence fit to ACT DR6 and Planck DR4 polarization, exploiting cross-power spectra between the two experiments — which break the $\alpha+\beta$ miscalibration degeneracy in independent ways (Planck via Galactic foreground $EB$, ACT via optics-model priors on $\alpha$, probing only $\ell>600$). Sampling 17 miscalibration angles, three dust-$EB$ amplitudes, and one $\beta$, the baseline gives $\beta=0.277^\circ\pm0.057^\circ$, excluding zero at $4.8\sigma$. Crucially, positive $EB$ appears in all three blocks separately: ACT×ACT ($0.207^\circ\pm0.073^\circ$), ACT×Planck ($0.178^\circ\pm0.078^\circ$, $2.3\sigma$), and Planck×Planck ($0.387^\circ\pm0.094^\circ$). A conservative dust-mitigation test — masking Planck to 30% Galactic cut, keeping only $\ell>600$, dropping foreground-dominated bands — still yields $0.236^\circ\pm0.067^\circ$ at $3.5\sigma$, so recovering $\beta=0$ requires both the $\alpha$ priors and the dust-$EB$ model to fail simultaneously.

Why it matters: a frequency-independent $\beta\neq0$ is the signature of an axion-like field coupled to electromagnetism via a Chern-Simons term — parity violation beyond ΛCDM, a direct window on the dark sector. Two independent hints now cross-correlate into the strongest signal yet. Caveats are candidly stated: the ACT×ACT block fits poorly (PTE 0.1%, an unexplained PA5 f090/f150 angle discrepancy the ACT team flagged), there is $1.5\sigma$ Planck–ACT tension in preferred $\beta$, the HFI priors are pre-flight, and the author's pipeline underestimates ACT $EB$ errors by ~7%. Unresolved systematics preclude a firm cosmological conclusion.

### [arXiv:2608.06731](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06731) — Laboratory-frame T-matrix and heavy quark drag in the quark-gluon plasma (Anurag Tiwari et al.)

Tiwari and He solve the in-medium two-body $T$-matrix directly in the medium rest (laboratory) frame rather than the pair center-of-mass frame, where the standard partial-wave reduction assumes the total pair-momentum $\vec P$ dependence is negligible. Since a thermal medium breaks Lorentz invariance and defines a preferred frame, physical observables must be built from lab-frame amplitudes. Exploiting the residual azimuthal symmetry about the $\vec P$-axis, they decompose the five-dimensional scattering equation into uncoupled integral equations for azimuthal components $T_m$, making it numerically tractable. For charm–light-quark scattering ($m_Q=1.3$ GeV, $m_q=0.4$ GeV, $T=190$ MeV, screened Cornell potential), the lab- and CM-frame amplitudes coincide at $P=0$ but diverge sharply as $P$ grows: in non-forward regions $|T_{\rm CM}|^2$ overshoots by 40–150% at $P=1$–1.5 GeV, and the relativistic normalization $R_E$ further distorts the forward peaks. Folded into the drag coefficient, the lab-frame result exceeds the CM baseline by 25–30% at $T=190$ MeV (low $p_Q$) and 30–40% at $T=258$ MeV across most momenta.

Why it matters: heavy-quark drag is the key transport parameter extracted from charm-hadron flow to characterize the near-ideal QGP. A previously unquantified 25–40% frame-mismatch bias directly reduces the theory uncertainty in inferring QGP transport properties — a concrete tightening. Caveats: the calculation uses constant quark widths ($\Gamma=100$–200 MeV, itself a 20–30% spread) rather than a self-consistent spectral function, treats only heavy–light-quark (not quark–gluon) scattering, and has not yet been propagated through Langevin transport to observables.
